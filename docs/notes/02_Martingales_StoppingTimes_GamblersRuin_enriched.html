Martingales and Stopping Times¶

1. A stopping time uses only current information¶

A filtration $(\mathcal F_n)_{n\geq0}$ records the information available through time $n$. A random time $\tau$ is a stopping time when

$$ \{\tau\leq n\}\in\mathcal F_n \qquad\text{for every }n. $$

Thus the decision to stop by time $n$ can be made from the information already observed. If $M=(M_n)$ is a martingale, then the stopped process

$$ M_n^\tau=M_{n\wedge\tau} $$

is again a martingale. This statement does not yet identify $E[M_\tau]$: passing from $M_{n\wedge\tau}$ to $M_\tau$ requires a convergence argument.

For a symmetric random walk $S_n=\sum_{j=1}^n\xi_j$ with $P(\xi_j=1)=P(\xi_j=-1)=1/2$, consider

$$ \tau=\inf\{n\geq0:S_n=A\text{ or }S_n=-B\}. $$

The boundary crossing is observable when it occurs, so $\tau$ is a stopping time.

Interpretation. Each path is drawn only until its boundary hit. The stopping rule depends on the observed path up to the current time, and the resulting stopped process remains inside the bounded interval $[-B,A]$.

2. Optional stopping solves bounded gambler's ruin¶

Because $S_{n\wedge\tau}$ is bounded by $\max(A,B)$, the family is uniformly integrable. Hence

$$ E[S_\tau]=E[S_0]=0. $$

Writing $u=P(S_\tau=A)$ gives

$$ 0=Au-B(1-u), \qquad u=\frac{B}{A+B}. $$

The process $S_n^2-n$ is also a martingale. Applying optional stopping to $\tau\wedge N$ and then passing to the limit under the finite-expectation argument for this bounded-interval exit yields

$$ E[\tau]=E[S_\tau^2]=AB. $$

3. A tilted martingale handles biased walks¶

Let $P(\xi_j=1)=p$, $P(\xi_j=-1)=q=1-p$, and suppose $p\neq1/2$. The walk $S_n$ has non-zero drift, but

$$ M_n=\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{S_n} $$

is a non-negative martingale. At the bounded-interval stopping time $\tau$, the stopped process $M_{n\wedge\tau}$ is bounded. Optional stopping therefore gives

$$ P(S_\tau=A) =\frac{1-(q/p)^B}{1-(q/p)^{A+B}}. $$

As $p\to1/2$, this expression converges to the symmetric probability $B/(A+B)$.

4. An unbounded stopping time exposes the missing hypothesis¶

For a symmetric random walk starting from zero, define

$$ \tau=\inf\{n\geq0:S_n=1\}. $$

This stopping time is finite almost surely and $S_\tau=1$, so $E[S_\tau]=1$. For every fixed $N$, however, the bounded stopping time $\tau\wedge N$ satisfies

$$ E[S_{\tau\wedge N}]=0. $$

There is no contradiction: the family $(S_{\tau\wedge N})_N$ is not uniformly integrable, so expectation cannot be interchanged with the almost-sure limit. 5. Continuous stopping and localization¶

If $M=(M_t)_{t\geq0}$ is a continuous martingale and $\tau$ is a stopping time, then $M_{t\wedge\tau}$ is again a martingale. For Brownian motion and the interval-exit time

$$ \tau_{A,B}=\inf\{t\geq0:B_t=A\text{ or }B_t=-B\}, $$

optional stopping applied with the required integrability gives

$$ P(B_{\tau_{A,B}}=A)=\frac{B}{A+B}, \qquad E[\tau_{A,B}]=AB. $$

The simulation observes Brownian motion only at times $k\Delta t$. It therefore computes the grid-monitored exit

$$ \tau_{A,B}^{\Delta t} =\inf\{k\Delta t:B_{k\Delta t}\geq A\text{ or }B_{k\Delta t}\leq-B\}, $$

which can miss a crossing between observations and can overshoot the boundary. The difference from the continuous-time benchmark is discretisation and monitoring error.

This stopping principle also underlies localization. A process $M$ is a local martingale when there are stopping times $T_n\uparrow\infty$ such that every $M^{T_n}$ is a martingale. Passing from the stopped martingales to a global martingale requires an additional condition such as domination by an integrable random variable.
