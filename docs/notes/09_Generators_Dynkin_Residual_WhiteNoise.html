Generators & Dynkin’s Formula¶

This chapter treats the generator as a microscope for small time, and Dynkin’s formula as what you get after you integrate that microscope over time.

The core trick is:

Start with a process $X_t$.
Pick a “test function” $f$.
Then subtract the predictable drift from $f(X_t)$ until what remains behaves like noise (a local martingale).

That “noise part” is what we will deliberately interpret as a kind of residual (in the regression sense) — a term which, after conditioning on the past, has mean $0$ and behaves “white-noise-ish” at small scales.

We’ll do this twice:

  1. Diffusions (driven by Brownian motion): noise looks like $dB_t$ (formal derivative = white noise).
  2. Pure jump Markov processes: noise looks like “jump minus compensator”.

Along the way, you’ll see why generators are the natural bridge between:

  • stochastic calculus (Itô / semimartingales),
  • Markov semigroups,
  • PDEs (backward/forward equations).

1. The diffusion generator: “what $f(X_t)$ wants to do on average”¶

Assume a 1D diffusion

$$dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t,t)\,dB_t.$$

The (time-dependent) generator $L_t$ is the second-order differential operator

$$(L_t f)(x,t) = \tfrac12 \sigma^2(x,t)\,\partial_{xx}f(x,t) \;+\; \mu(x,t)\,\partial_x f(x,t).$$

Compact Itô = “drift + residual”¶

For $f\in C^{2,1}$,

$$df(X_t,t)=\big((L_t f)(X_t,t)+\partial_t f(X_t,t)\big)\,dt +\partial_x f(X_t,t)\,\sigma(X_t,t)\,dB_t.$$

So define the drift-stripped process

$$M_t^{(f)} := f(X_t,t) - f(X_0,0) - \int_0^t \big((L_u f)+\partial_u f\big)(X_u,u)\,du.$$

Under standard integrability conditions (e.g. bounded $\partial_x f$), this is a martingale; in general it’s at least a local martingale.

Interpret $M^{(f)}$ as: what’s left after you remove everything predictable from $f(X_t,t)$.

2. Local martingale = residual: the “innovation” you couldn’t predict¶

A helpful (not literally true) mental model is:

$$\xi(t):=\frac{dB_t}{dt}$$

and call $\xi$ white noise.

Then the diffusion reads like a random ODE:

$$\dot X_t = \mu(X_t,t)+\sigma(X_t,t)\,\xi(t),$$

and the Itô integral is the agreed meaning of “integrating against $\xi$”.

Regression analogy (surprisingly faithful)¶

$$f(X_t,t)=f(X_0,0)+\underbrace{\int_0^t\big((L_u f)+\partial_u f\big)(X_u,u)\,du}_{\text{predictable drift}} +\underbrace{M_t^{(f)}}_{\text{residual}}.$$

The bracket / quadratic variation of $M^{(f)}$ acts like a variance budget for the residual.

3. Visual experiment (diffusion): OU and “whiteness” of the residual¶

Simulate OU:

$$dX_t=-\kappa X_t\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t.$$

Choose $f(x)=x^2$.

Compute its generator:

  • $f'(x)=2x$, $f''(x)=2$
  • $\mu(x)=-\kappa x$, $\sigma(x)=\sigma$

$$(Lf)(x)=\tfrac12\sigma^2\cdot 2 +(-\kappa x)\cdot 2x = \sigma^2-2\kappa x^2.$$

Then

$$M_t=f(X_t)-f(X_0)-\int_0^t (Lf)(X_s)\,ds$$

should look like noise:

  • mean ~ $0$ across paths
  • increment correlations ~ $0$
  • standardized increments ~ $N(0,1)$

4. Dynkin with stopping: “stop before things blow up”¶

Many “nice” expressions are only local martingales globally. So we localize with a stopping time.

Stop OU at exit from $[-a,a]$:

$$\tau := \inf\{t:|X_t|\ge a\}\wedge T.$$

Then (heuristically) the stopped residual should satisfy:

$$E[M_\tau]\approx 0.$$

E[M_tau] ≈ 0.0001415208600788255
Std(M_tau) ≈ 0.14474744231981676
Fraction stopped early (tau<T): 0.9246

5. Jump processes: same template, different noise¶

For a Markov jump process with intensity $\lambda(x)$ and jump size $\xi(x)$:

$$(Lf)(x)=\lambda(x)\,E[f(x+\xi(x))-f(x)].$$

And the structural fact is:

$$M_t^{(f)} := f(X_t)-f(X_0)-\int_0^t (Lf)(X_s)\,ds$$

is a local martingale.

There is also a predictable bracket identity:

$$\langle M^{(f)}\rangle_t=\int_0^t \Gamma f(X_s)\,ds,\qquad \Gamma f = L(f^2)-2fLf.$$

We simulate a compound Poisson process: constant intensity $\lambda$, i.i.d. jumps $Y$.

6. Martingale problem viewpoint: define the process by its residuals¶

A major “upgrade” in probability thinking is:

A Markov process can be characterized by a collection of associated martingales.

In practice:

  • Write down a candidate generator $L$.
  • Demand that $f(X_t)-f(X_0)-\int_0^t Lf(X_s)\,ds$ is a (local) martingale for a rich class of $f$.
  • If uniqueness holds, you’ve pinned down the law of $X$.

Operator / semigroup form¶

If $Q_t f(x)=E_x[f(X_t)]$, then a canonical identity is

$$Q_t f = f + \int_0^t Q_s(Lf)\,ds,$$

which is Dynkin’s formula in functional-analytic clothing.

7. Bonus bridge: generator ⇒ PDE (Brownian ⇒ heat equation)¶

For Brownian motion in $\mathbb{R}^d$, $L=\tfrac12\Delta$. For

$$u(t,x)=E_x[\varphi(B_t)],$$

one gets $\partial_t u = \tfrac12 \Delta u$.

This is the cleanest example of the general theme:

Generators are PDE operators, and Dynkin/Itô translate between “PDE truth” and “martingale truth”.
