Itô’s Formula: The Chain Rule That Remembers Quadratic Variation¶

Itô’s formula becomes intuitive once you accept one geometric fact:

Brownian motion has quadratic variation $[B,B]_t = t$, so “$(dB)^2$ behaves like $dt$”.

Roadmap¶

  1. Core statements: $f(B_t)$, $f(t,B_t)$
  2. Itô processes and the general chain rule
  3. Semimartingale form: $df(X)=f'(X)dX+\frac{1}{2}f''(X)d[X,X]$
  4. The geometric reason: Taylor expansion + quadratic variation
  5. Visual experiments:
    • $f(x)=x^2$: see the correction term appear
    • exponential martingale: $dt$ cancellation by design
    • time-dependent $f(t,x)$: PDE $\Rightarrow$ martingale
    • GBM/log drift shift $-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2$ (finance classic)

1. Statements you should memorize¶

Brownian (time-homogeneous)¶

For $f\in C^2(\mathbb R)$,

$$f(B_t)-f(0)=\int_0^t f'(B_s)\,dB_s+\frac12\int_0^t f''(B_s)\,ds.$$

Time-dependent¶

For $f\in C^{1,2}(\mathbb R_+\times\mathbb R)$,

$$f(t,B_t)-f(0,0)=\int_0^t \partial_x f(s,B_s)\,dB_s+\int_0^t \partial_s f(s,B_s)\,ds+\frac12\int_0^t \partial_{xx}^2 f(s,B_s)\,ds.$$

Only one thing is “new” vs classical calculus: the $\tfrac12(\cdot)dt$ correction.

2. Semimartingale form¶

For a continuous semimartingale $X$,

$$df(X_t)=f'(X_t)\,dX_t+\frac12 f''(X_t)\,d[X,X]_t.$$

  • If $[X,X]_t=0$ (finite variation), you recover ordinary calculus.
  • For Brownian motion, $d[B,B]_t=dt$, hence the Itô correction term.

3. Why the correction term exists¶

On a partition $0=t_0<\dots<t_n=t$,

$$f(B_{t_{i+1}})-f(B_{t_i}) \approx f'(B_{t_i})\Delta B_i+\frac12 f''(B_{t_i})(\Delta B_i)^2.$$

Summing and letting mesh→0:

  • the first term becomes the Itô integral,
  • the second term becomes $\tfrac12\int_0^t f''(B_s)\,ds$ because $\sum(\Delta B)^2\to t$.

7. Experiment C — time-dependent $f(t,x)$: PDE ⇒ martingale¶

If $f_t+\tfrac12 f_{xx}=0$, then $f(t,B_t)$ has no drift term.

Example: $e^{B_t-t/2}$ has mean 1.

8. Experiment D — GBM/log drift shift¶

If $\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t$, then

$$d(\log S_t)=(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t.$$
