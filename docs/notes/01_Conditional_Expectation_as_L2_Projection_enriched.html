Conditional Expectation as an $L^2$ Projection¶

1. Conditioning selects the part measurable with the information¶

Let $(\Omega,\Sigma,P)$ be a probability space and let $\Sigma_0\subseteq\Sigma$ be a sub-$\sigma$-algebra. For $Y\in L^2(\Omega,\Sigma,P)$, the conditional expectation $E(Y\mid\Sigma_0)$ is the unique element of $L^2(\Omega,\Sigma_0,P)$ satisfying

$$ \int_A Y\,dP=\int_A E(Y\mid\Sigma_0)\,dP \qquad\text{for every }A\in\Sigma_0. $$

Conditioning on a random variable means conditioning on its generated information: $E(Y\mid X)=E(Y\mid\sigma(X))$. To distinguish the exact object from a finite-sample estimate, consider

$$ X\sim\operatorname{Unif}[-1,1], \qquad Y=X^2+\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2), \quad \varepsilon\ \text{independent of }X. $$

Then $E(Y\mid X)=X^2$. If $B_m(X)$ records only which of $m$ equal bins contains $X$ and $\Sigma_m=\sigma(B_m(X))$, the coarser exact projection is

$$ E(Y\mid\Sigma_m) =E(X^2\mid\Sigma_m) =\frac{a_j^2+a_jb_j+b_j^2}{3} \quad\text{on the bin }[a_j,b_j]. $$

The sample mean within a bin estimates this step function; it is not the exact conditional expectation given $X$.

2. The residual is orthogonal to every measurable predictor¶

The space $L^2(\Omega,\Sigma_0,P)$ is a closed subspace of $L^2(\Omega,\Sigma,P)$. Conditional expectation is the orthogonal projection onto that subspace. If

$$ m_0=E(Y\mid\Sigma_0), \qquad R=Y-m_0, $$

then

$$ E(RZ)=0 \qquad\text{for every }Z\in L^2(\Omega,\Sigma_0,P). $$

For the finite partition $\Sigma_m$, it is enough to read this relation against the bin indicators. On each bin $A_j$,

$$ E(R\mathbf 1_{A_j})=0, $$

or equivalently $E(R\mid A_j)=0$ whenever $P(A_j)>0$.

3. Orthogonality gives the best $L^2$ predictor¶

For every $Z\in L^2(\Omega,\Sigma_0,P)$,

$$ E[(Y-Z)^2] =E[(Y-m_0)^2]+E[(m_0-Z)^2], \qquad m_0=E(Y\mid\Sigma_0). $$

The cross term vanishes because $m_0-Z$ is $\Sigma_0$-measurable and $Y-m_0$ is orthogonal to that subspace. The identity makes the minimizer and its uniqueness immediate.

For the eight-bin model, take

$$ Z_\alpha=m_0+\alpha\{m_0-E(Y)\}. $$

This stays $\Sigma_8$-measurable. Its exact risk is a quadratic function of $\alpha$ with its minimum at zero.

4. Nested information produces iterated projections¶

If $\Sigma_1\subseteq\Sigma_0\subseteq\Sigma$, the tower property gives

$$ E\{E(Y\mid\Sigma_0)\mid\Sigma_1\} =E(Y\mid\Sigma_1). $$

Thus projecting first onto a finer information set and then onto a coarser one gives the coarse projection directly. For $X\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]$ and

$$ Y=g(X)+\varepsilon, \qquad g(x)=\sin(2\pi x)+\frac{x^2}{2}, \qquad E(\varepsilon\mid X)=0, $$

let $\Sigma_k$ be generated by the $2^k$ equal subintervals of $[0,1]$. Each $E(Y\mid\Sigma_k)$ is the exact average of $g$ on its bin. Refinement enlarges the projection space and reduces the approximation error.

5. Projection separates explained and residual variance¶

Assume $Y\in L^2$ and let $m_0=E(Y\mid\Sigma_0)$. Since $Y-E(Y)=(Y-m_0)+(m_0-E(Y))$ is an orthogonal decomposition,

$$ \operatorname{Var}(Y) =E\{\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid\Sigma_0)\} +\operatorname{Var}\{E(Y\mid\Sigma_0)\}. $$

The first term is residual variance after conditioning; the second is variance resolved by the information set. For $Y=X^2+\varepsilon$, the total variance is

$$ \operatorname{Var}(Y) =\operatorname{Var}(X^2)+\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) =\frac{4}{45}+\sigma_\varepsilon^2. $$

Refining equal-width bins moves variance from the residual component to the explained component while preserving their sum.
